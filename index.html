Why an evolutionary view of behaviour matters in consulting

Behavioural economics

Clients are not rational either

The evolutionary logic

Every organisation is run by people whose judgement uses the same fast shortcuts. Good analysis has to expect them, in the client and in the consultant.

In consulting

  • Confirmation bias: teams look for evidence that backs the plan, so I write competing hypotheses before any data.
  • Sunk cost: past spending distorts the next decision, so options are compared on future value only.
  • Planning fallacy and overconfidence: timelines and forecasts are given as ranges, not single numbers.
  • Status quo bias: doing nothing feels safe, so the cost of inaction is made explicit.
Cognitive ease

Simple recommendations get adopted

The evolutionary logic

Attention and working memory are limited and costly. A recommendation that is hard to grasp is a recommendation that is hard to act on.

In consulting

  • One clear so-what first, then the supporting evidence (answer-first storytelling).
  • Three priorities rather than twelve, ranked by impact and effort.
  • One message per slide, with the title carrying the conclusion.
  • Reducing friction in change: a small first step and a pilot beat a big-bang plan.
Conformity

Change is a social process

The evolutionary logic

People look to peers before they commit, especially when the outcome is uncertain. A strategy spreads through people, not through slides.

In consulting

  • Value is not only about a sound analysis, it is about a story that people repeat to each other.
  • Champions and early adopters make the new way look normal.
  • Pilots and reference cases act as social proof for the rest of the organisation.
  • Aligning key stakeholders one by one before the big meeting avoids surprises in the room.

Why an evolutionary view of behaviour matters in marketing

Behavioural economics

Humans are not rational

The evolutionary logic

Judgement runs on fast shortcuts that evolved for survival in small groups, not for spreadsheets. They are efficient most of the time and predictably wrong some of the time.

In marketing

  • Loss aversion: a loss weighs more than an equal gain, so free returns and money-back guarantees remove the fear of losing.
  • Anchoring: the first number seen frames every later one, hence the crossed-out original price.
  • Decoy effect: a third, weaker option makes the target option look like the obvious choice.
  • Scarcity: "only 3 left" signals value because rare things were worth competing for.
Cognitive ease

The brain dislikes complexity

The evolutionary logic

The brain is costly to run, so it saves effort wherever it can. Every extra step, field or option is a cost that people avoid paying.

In marketing

  • Reducing friction is the key: fewer clicks, fewer form fields, one clear next step.
  • Sensible defaults: most people keep the option that is already chosen.
  • Fewer choices: a short menu converts better than a long one (choice overload).
  • Progressive disclosure: show the essentials first and the detail on request.
Conformity

We follow the group

The evolutionary logic

For a social species, copying others is a cheap way to learn what is safe, good or valuable. We also remember stories far better than features.

In marketing

  • Value is not only about producing a good product, it is about producing a good story.
  • Social proof: reviews, "most popular" labels and user counts reduce uncertainty.
  • Belonging: brands work as signals of identity, a tribe people want to be seen in.
  • Narrative: an origin, a mission and a shared meaning make a product worth talking about.
